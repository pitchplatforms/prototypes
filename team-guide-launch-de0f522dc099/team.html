Launch 2 · the team model

The brain runs like a product. You’re its users — and its co-authors.

The brain is a product you use, not one you maintain. It is distributed read-only, so every laptop holds the same copy. Updates ask before they apply, so nothing lands without your yes. And it listens back through three channels, so what you run into this week becomes next week’s update. Nothing changes under you silently, and there’s nothing you can break.

The contract

One brain, read-only, updated only when you say yes

Here’s what you get out of it: every clone is identical. There’s nothing to break, no drift between laptops, and never a “whose version is right?” The owner pushes; you pull — and pulling is always your call.

This is what your session start looks like. When an update is waiting, the brain shows you a short summary in plain language and asks before touching anything — nothing downloads until you say yes:

Brain update available Session start
1Updates available — the owner published changes since your last pull.
2Here’s what changed (plain language):
• The PR-review skill now flags gateway routes that don’t match a new API endpoint.
• Setup docs updated for the one-command local boot.
3Apply? Nothing has downloaded yet — your copy is untouched until you choose.
Yes, apply Not now

On a yes the brain does a clean fast-forward pull — no surprises, no merge to untangle. On not now it does nothing and asks again next time. Every behaviour change is written up in CHANGELOG.md, so you can always read exactly what moved and why.

The one rule to remember: don’t push — route it

Learned something team-wide? A better way, a wrong doc, a rough edge? Send it back through a channel — don’t push a change to the brain. That’s what keeps every clone identical. (ADRs are the one exception you publish yourself — see ADRs’ new home.)

The loop

Your experience becomes next week’s update

The brain improves in a weekly loop, and you’re part of what turns it. What you run into flows back up. The owner triages it. It comes back down as an update you’re asked to apply. The more you feed it, the better it gets — for everyone on the next pull.

  1. 1You pull the brain down. One shared copy lands on your laptop, read-only.
  2. 2Your experience flows back up. A bug, an idea, or a weekly pulse rating — whatever you hit becomes signal.
  3. 3The owner triages it. Weekly: what’s worth a change gets one; the rest gets a reason.
  4. 4The update flows back down. It returns as a change you’re asked to apply — and the loop turns again.

Three channels — the ten-second routing rule

You never have to guess where something goes. Ten seconds, three doors:

Something broke / a wrong doc

Run /brain-feedback

It files a GitHub issue on pitchplatforms/ai-setup. The agent gathers the context, drafts the issue, and files it only on your yes.

An idea, friction, or praise

The same /brain-feedback skill

Same door, different template (the brain-feedback template). Half-formed is fine — the agent shapes it with you before anything is filed.

The weekly pulse

Once a week, at session start

A 1–5 rating plus one rotating question. Under a minute, skippable, permanently opt-out-able, and never mid-task. More below.

The one hard boundary

Keep two trackers apart and you’ll never misfile:

brain feedback → GitHub issues   product work → ClickUp Never the other way round — a broken skill is not a ClickUp ticket, and a product bug is not a GitHub issue (ADR-0013).
The relay

Shipping is a relay: you pass a clean baton, one integrator lands it

On the product side (the ClickUp lane above), code reaches staging in two hands. Authors hand over small, finished changes on branches of their own. One integrator per feature carries the whole thing across the line. You never touch a shared branch, so there’s nothing you can break.

The author — that’s most of us

You work on your own topic branch and open one small PR into the feature branch; the feature owner reviews and merges it. That’s the whole baton — small, finished, reviewed. And there are three moves an author never makes: never rebase onto staging, never force-push, never resolve a conflict with “use mine”. If anything is flagged, you hand off — that’s the move.

The integrator — one engineer per feature

The engineer who owns the feature branch. They merge the authors’ PRs, then make the one risky move the whole workflow exists to isolate: replaying the feature onto the latest staging and opening the single PR that lands it. Dangerous work lives in exactly one pair of hands — never yours.

The handoff label is the system working

When a staging check trips, the PR waits as a draft with the needs-engineer-integration label, and an engineer takes over on GitHub. Nobody dropped the baton — the risky leg was routed to the runner equipped for it. That is the design.

Follow one change, step by step

That’s what pairs with what. This map is the same relay in time — it follows the FRC-815 lane you just traced, one small change: ticket FRC-815, a story under feature FRC-813. Pick your journey, then click a step to see who drives it, what you’ll see, and the exact convention. Arrow keys work too. Green means that step is yours; grey steps happen without you.

Step 1 · You

The ticket is ready in ClickUp

FRC-815 (“show reward on portfolio”) is a story under feature FRC-813 (“referral rewards”). Its description says what to build and how you’ll know it’s done. You type /ticket-to-ship FRC-815 and the agent takes it from there. The ticket’s ID now follows the change everywhere: branch name, commit messages, PR title. That’s how anyone traces code back to its ticket.

ClickUp FRC-815 → in progress when the build starts
Step 2 · You — the agent does the typing

A branch that is yours alone

The agent cuts a branch just for this change, off the feature branch. Think of it as your private copy. Nobody else works on it, so nothing you do here can touch anyone else’s work. Every save (a “commit”) is stamped with the ticket’s ID.

Your branch — named after your ticket <type>/<SUBTASK-REF>-<slug> e.g. feat/FRC-815-reward-display
Every commit message — the ticket being built <REF> <short lowercase description> e.g. FRC-815 show reward on portfolio

The prefix is the branch’s type. Four types, and every branch uses one:

feat/new feature work — also the feature’s shared branch, the unit that ships
fix/a bug fix with no parent feature — a “feature of one”, same rules
hotfix/an urgent fix for production
chore/tooling or setup work, not product code

Your own branch is typed the same way — <type>/<REF>-<slug>. The type lives in the branch name, so commit messages never need one.

Step 3 · You approve — the agent opens it

Your small PR into the feature branch

A PR (pull request) means “please review and merge my branch”. The agent fills it in and asks you before opening. The reviewer is the feature owner, an engineer. The moment the PR opens, your ticket flips to pr on its own.

Then one tidy-up. While building, the agent gave itself disposable working copies of the code on your laptop. Once your PR is up, it offers to clean them up — it always asks before deleting anything, and work already saved on GitHub is never lost.

Step 4 · The feature owner

Review, then squash-merge

The feature owner reviews your PR and squash-merges it. “Squash” means your work lands as one clean commit in the feature branch. Your branch is now done; the next change starts a fresh one. The feature branch collects everyone’s merged changes, and it only changes through reviewed PRs like yours.

The feature branch — named after the feature’s ticket feat/<REF>-<kebab> e.g. feat/FRC-813-referral-rewards

One move takes the whole feature to staging

The integrator is the engineer who owns the feature branch — the same person who reviewed your PR. They replay the finished feature onto the latest staging, then open one PR for the whole feature. Tony reviews it. It merges with a merge commit, never squash, so every commit keeps its ticket ID.

The detour · only if a safety check trips

The PR parks itself and an engineer takes over

Sometimes staging moved and the merge isn’t clean. A safety check catches that before anything merges. The PR waits as a draft, gets the needs-engineer-integration label, and an engineer resolves it on GitHub. This is the system working. If no check trips, this step is skipped entirely.

Step 6 · Gated promotion

Merged to staging, then promoted rung by rung

The feature merges into staging and the ticket flips to staging. From there the whole feature is promoted staging → uat2 → master, with a release check at every rung. Nothing skips a rung, and a fix re-enters through the same feature branch.

Engineer who’d rather skip the agent entirely? Same relay, same rules, typed by hand — the manual lane below.

Worried you’ll break someone else’s work? You can’t.

Three protections are built into the setup:

1. Your branch is yours aloneEvery change gets its own branch, named after its ticket. Nobody else works on it.
2. Shared code only changes by reviewed PRNothing lands until another person reviews your change and merges it.
3. The risky move is never yoursOne integrator replays the feature onto staging. Anything unclear gets parked as a draft PR for an engineer.

And the boundary above still holds: this is product work, so it runs on ClickUp tickets — never GitHub issues (ADR-0013).

ADRs’ new home

The one thing you publish yourself

Architecture Decision Records now live in pitchplatforms/ADRs — the one repo the team has Write on. Everything else routes through the owner; ADRs you push directly. Before the moves, two things worth knowing: what an ADR actually is, and how one gets made.

What’s an ADR?

A short, numbered note that records one decision: what we chose, why, and what it replaces. All of them sit in that one shared repo, so a settled question stays settled — the next person reads the number instead of re-arguing it. Anyone can pull one up by its number.

You don’t write them — decisions become them

Nobody sits down to “write an ADR”. When a real decision settles during a working session (say, a grill-with-docs grilling), the agent offers to record it, drafts it, and publishes it with the script below. And you don’t need an agent at all — the non-technical path never touches git:

  1. Notice a decision was made — in a meeting, a chat, anywhere.
  2. Fill the short form — “Log a decision or change” on pitchplatforms/ai-setup, in your own words.
  3. The owner’s sweep drafts it — your plain words become a proper structured draft.
  4. The owner approves — only then is it published, numbered, citable by everyone.

The same split as the journey map above: the tooling does the technical part.

Reference one by number anywhere — a PR body, a ClickUp ticket. Needs nothing local (e.g. ADR-0022).

The manual lane

Prefer to work without the AI? Same rules, by hand

The agent is optional. The conventions are not. Everything above runs exactly the same if you never touch the agent — it’s the relay you already traced in the ClickUp ↔ git mapping and the step-by-step map, typed by hand. Seven steps, start to finish.

  1. Start from the ticket — nothing changes here

    Work still begins on a ClickUp ticket, and the ticket’s ref (e.g. FRC-815) follows the change everywhere: branch name, every commit, the PR. That ref is what ties git back to the tracker — with or without an agent.

  2. Feature owners: cut the feature branch, record it in ClickUp

    One branch per feature: feat/<REF>-<kebab-title>, cut off the product default. Record it in the ticket’s Feature Branch field when the ticket is created. Working a sub-task? Read that field to find the branch — never guess the name.

  3. Cut your own topic branch, commit with the ref in front

    Your branch is <type>/<REF>-<slug> off the feature branch — feat/, fix/, hotfix/ or chore/, whichever matches the ticket. Every commit subject is <REF> <short lowercase description>. Stage your files by name — never git add -A.

  4. Open a draft PR into the feature branch

    Title <REF> <short lowercase description>; the body’s first line is <REF>[PR] — that line flips your ticket to pr when the PR opens. Same automation, no AI involved. Reviewer is the feature owner; they review and squash-merge. Never push to the feature branch or staging directly, and never force-push.

  5. Integrators: one move to staging, then the ladder

    One engineer replays the finished feature onto the latest staging — the one git rebase --onto, run once — and opens the one PR for the whole feature: the Feature’s ref in the title, its <REF>[PR] token in the body (plus tokens for any trailing sub-tasks), merged with a merge commit, never squash. From there the feature promotes staging → uat2 → master, release-check gated at every rung. If a safety check trips, the PR parks itself as a draft with needs-engineer-integration and an engineer resolves it on GitHub.

  6. Made a decision worth recording? File the form

    No git needed: file “Log a decision or change” on pitchplatforms/ai-setup, in plain words — the same form the non-technical path above uses. The owner-run sweep (weekly, or at promotion time) drafts it properly; the owner approves; only then is it published as a numbered ADR. Comfortable with the script instead? A hand-written DRAFT-<slug>.md + bin/adr-publish.sh works too — the script assigns the number, never pick one yourself.

  7. Changelogs: your commits already wrote the draft

    Nobody hand-maintains a changelog ticket by ticket. At promotion time the same owner-run sweep drafts the entry from what actually shipped — commits grouped by their leading <REF>, one more reason step 3’s commit convention matters — and the owner reviews before anything lands. Writing an entry by hand is fine too: a date, a plain-language title, and “What changed.” — the exact shape is in docs/changelog-standard.md.

The copy-paste cheat-sheet

docs/git-workflow-human.md in the brain exists for exactly this reader: the author steps above as commands you can paste, plus the hard rules — never rebase onto staging yourself, never force-push, and on any conflict, stop and ask. The full picture, both lanes, is docs/git-workflow.md.

The weekly pulse

A one-minute rating that builds a track record

Your weekly rating is how friction gets heard. Over time it builds a track record of how the brain is working for the team — a signal the owner can act on. It takes under a minute, and it’s the lightest channel of the three.

This week’s brain pulse Session start
How well did the brain work for you this week?
12345
This week’s question: Which skill saved you the most time — or wasted it?
Submit Skip this week Stop asking

Three responses, always offered up front: Submit a 1–5 rating plus the rotating question’s answer; Skip this week (it asks again next week); or Stop asking (permanent, honoured forever — and undoable by you whenever you want it back).

  • once a week at most
  • session-start only, never mid-task
  • a skip is remembered
  • “stop asking” is honoured forever
  • under a minute
For the curious: what happens under the hood

You just answer the box in conversation — there’s no command to type. Behind it, Claude records the outcome with bin/pulse-submit.sh (--skip and --stop included), so your rating, skip, or opt-out is remembered across sessions.

Everything is operator-stamped — credit, not surveillance. Issues, pulses, ADR publishes, and PRs all carry your name, never anonymous. Seats are shared, so a name on the record is how you get the credit for what you sent — and how the owner can follow up with the right person. It’s traceability, not a watch.

You’re not stuck

If something’s off

Four things people ask — each has a one-step answer.

adr-get fails or 404s

You likely need collaborator access on pitchplatforms/ADRs. Ask the owner to add you — citing by number never needs access, only reading a body does.

I disagree with a brain change

Say so: file feedback via /brain-feedback, or make the case durable — write an ADR (DRAFT-<slug>.md → bin/adr-publish.sh). Disagreement is signal the loop wants.

I want to stop the pulse

Choose “stop asking” once. It’s a single honoured choice — it never asks again. Change your mind later and you can turn it back on yourself.
