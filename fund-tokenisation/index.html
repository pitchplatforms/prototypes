The life of a tokenised fund,
from first filing to final buyback

7 chapters ≈ 9 minutes All figures in USD

Seven short chapters walk the entire life of a tokenised fund on the platform — shown across two sibling funds, one newly created and one eight months into its life: the regulated intake, investor onboarding and custody, the raise, allotment and token minting, quarterly reporting and voting, secondary trading, and finally redemption — where the units are bought back, the cash lands, and the tokens are burned. Each chapter is a video tour of finished product screens; this page carries the story between them.

The platform is a licensed operator of regulated investment crowdfunding and secondary trading, supervised by its home securities regulator.

Chapter 1 · 1:23

A regulated intake for funds

01 Chapter 1 — A regulated intake for funds 1:23

Nusantara Micro Fund I enters the platform the way every issuer does: through a gated intake. Four gates — Fundraise Application, Due Diligence, Offer Creation, Create Campaign — each reviewed and approved item by item before anything can be published. The chapter walks the completed checklist, what the manager filed, the approved offer terms, the campaign story written on the fund template, and the same checklist from the back office where the reviews were done and the campaign was set live.

  • →An offer cannot go live without passing every gate — the checklist shows a review date and status on each item.
  • →The story template speaks fund, not startup: Strategy & Mandate, Portfolio & Pipeline, Fee Structure & Waterfall, Term & Liquidity, Manager & Track Record, Governance.
  • →The fund's offer carries no valuation and no equity percentage — it is priced per unit against minimum and maximum targets.
Chapter 2 · 0:53

Investor onboarding and custody

02 Chapter 2 — Investor onboarding and custody 0:53

One onboarding covers everything an investor will do — subscribing, trading, and holding tokens. The platform gates investing until identity is verified and an investor classification is assigned, and each classification carries annual investment limits the platform enforces automatically. A custodial token wallet is created for every investor without ceremony: no seed phrase, no gas, no chain interface.

  • →Three classifications with hard annual limits, enforced at the moment of investment — not by policy, by software.
  • →The wallet shows balances, per-token holdings and a full transaction history; withdrawals go only to whitelisted addresses with confirmation and platform approval.
  • →The custody layer never surfaces — investors see tokens and values, never keys.
Chapter 3 · 1:22

A campaign page that speaks fund

03 Chapter 3 — A campaign page that speaks fund 1:22

The platform treats a fund as a different kind of issuer, not a company with odd labels. On the opportunity list the fund card shows raised, minimum target and investors — and no valuation — beside a company card that has one. The campaign page speaks in units and lots, its sidebar drops the equity and valuation rows, its story sections are the fund set from Chapter 1, and its document pack opens as real PDFs. Investors ask questions in public and the manager answers under a Creator badge. A subscription is shown as a fact: ten lots, $10,000, declarations ticked, and the row it produced on the investor's dashboard.

  • →Same rails, different vocabulary: 1 unit $1.00 · 1,000 units per lot · minimum 5 lots — and no valuation anywhere.
  • →The document pack — information memorandum, partnership agreement, fee schedule, risk disclosure — downloads as genuine documents.
  • →Q&A is on the record, attributed, and stays with the campaign for its life.
Chapter 4 · 1:08

Allotment: one register, tokens to match

04 Chapter 4 — Allotment: one register, tokens to match 1:08

The fund closes at its $500,000 minimum with 38 investors. The issuer's investor book is finalised, and allotment writes it into the platform's register: one unit class, NUSA, 500,000 units, every holder with a quantity, direct or nominee ownership, and a digital certificate. Tokens are then minted 1:1 against the register into each holder's custody wallet. The chapter ends where every change of hands in this walkthrough ends — on the investor's dashboard, units and tokens side by side, equal.

  • →The register is the authoritative record; the token is its representation. That ordering is the design.
  • →Every holding carries a digital certificate the back office can view and regenerate.
  • →10,000 units in the register ↔ 10,000 tokens in the wallet, from the first minute.

The same lifecycle, shown on two funds

The later stages need time to have passed — a track record to report, a portfolio old enough to value, a trading history that makes selling plausible. So chapters 1–4 follow Nusantara Micro Fund I, created from nothing on screen, and chapters 5–7 follow Meridian Logistics Fund I — the same kind of fund, closed in December 2025 at $800,000 with 46 investors, 800,000 units at $1.00, now eight months into its life. Nothing else changes: same intake, same register, same custody.

Chapter 5 · 1:17

Reporting, NAV and investor voting

05 Chapter 5 — Reporting, NAV and investor voting 1:17

Reporting continues for the life of the fund, on the same page investors subscribed on. Meridian publishes quarterly reports and a net asset value — $1.42 per unit as at 30 June 2026 — and, after selling two assets, announces a tender offer to buy back up to 40% of units at full NAV. The vote runs on the platform: the register decides who votes, the platform chases every unvoted holder at each login, the decision page states the holder's own position and payout in plain terms, and a confirmed vote is final. Voters see their own vote and the published outcome — never a running tally.

  • →The voter roster comes from the register — governance and record-keeping are the same system.
  • →The platform reminds the investor at every login until the vote is cast, and a cast vote cannot be changed.
  • →The decision page computes the holder's own numbers: units held, cash payable, total return.
Chapter 6 · 1:50

A regulated secondary market

06 Chapter 6 — A regulated secondary market 1:50

Meridian's units trade under market controls: a listed instrument with a ticker, a live order book, limit orders only, fees shown before confirmation, and a password on the final step. One trade is followed end to end — a holder's resting sell of 3,000 units at $1.35 is lifted by a buyer, the back office shows order against matched quantity and quoted against final price, and the print lands in recent trades. Then the part that matters: the register moves 10,000 → 7,000 and 0 → 3,000, the tokens move with it, and both dashboards show the new positions the same day. (Why sell at $1.35 with a $1.42 tender open? Tender settlement is weeks away and acceptance is capped at 40% of units — some holders prefer certain cash now at a small discount.)

  • →The market trades at $1.35 against a published NAV of $1.42 — a ~5% discount, which is how private fund secondaries genuinely price.
  • →Every fill settles back to the register on trade date — the record the tokens mirror is never stale.
  • →The buyer's wallet and register entry appear automatically — a new holder needs no setup.
Chapter 7 · 0:57

Redemption by tender

07 Chapter 7 — Redemption by tender 0:57

Redemption of a closed-end fund is the fund offering to buy its own units back — a tender, not an on-demand withdrawal, and not a market trade. Meridian's offer is priced at $1.42, full NAV, against a market that last traded about 5% below it — which is exactly why a holder tenders. The holder's decision page spells out the return; acceptance is recorded; the outcome is published. Settlement closes the loop: cash lands in the account, the tokens are burned, the units are extinguished, and the register created at allotment is decremented — 800,000 units become 488,000, and the token supply follows it down.

  • →Tender at 100% of NAV vs a market ~5% below — the economics answer "why would anyone tender?"
  • →Settlement is symmetrical with issuance: mint on allotment, burn on redemption, the register authoritative at both ends.
  • →Holders who decline simply keep their units — participation is a choice, recorded per holder.
